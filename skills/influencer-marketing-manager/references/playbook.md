# Influencer Marketing Playbook

Use this playbook as adaptive decision guidance. Start at the user's current lifecycle stage, define the result that matters now, choose evidence in proportion to the decision, and let observed outcomes refine the next move.

## 1. Frame the work

Translate the request into a working business hypothesis:

> For **[target audience]**, **[creator type]** in **[real content scene]** can encourage **[target behavior]** because **[evidence or insight]**.

Capture:

- primary outcome and any secondary outcome;
- audience, market, language, product, timing, and resources;
- must-haves, preferences, and assumptions;
- current lifecycle stage and known relationship state;
- authority for research, preparation, outreach, spending, and commercial commitments;
- the observable signal that would count as progress in this stage.

Ask only questions that change the next action. If the missing information affects a reversible first step, state a working assumption and test it with a small sample. If it affects spend, rights, reputation, or an external promise, bring the choice to the user.

## 2. Discover creators in two passes

Discovery has two different jobs. The first pass protects coverage and efficiency; the second protects decision quality. Keep their inputs, costs, and outputs distinct.

### 2.1 Form the coarse-screen query

Turn the hypothesis into structured search dimensions:

- platform and format;
- market, country, language, and audience location;
- topic, category, and concrete use scene;
- recency and basic activity;
- rough reach or performance range;
- contact or collaboration signals when available.

Use the available creator-intelligence capability at runtime for command syntax, current fields, quotas, and result identifiers. Run a small set of complementary queries instead of one over-specific query that hides adjacent supply. Keep each query's purpose visible so later results can be compared.

### 2.2 Pass A — coarse screen (high recall, low cost)

Use search results and structured fields to form a broad candidate pool. Apply the active project's must-haves and the experienced baseline when the project has no rule. Typical checks include platform, market/language, apparent category, recent activity, rough format mix, and obvious identity or duplicate signals.

Deduplicate by a stable creator or channel ID; retain the source URL and query snapshot for every candidate. Prepare a pool large enough to absorb normal qualification attrition, then stop expanding when additional results are less useful than deeper review. Quality and supply determine the final size.

Each coarse candidate card contains:

```text
creator/channel identity and stable ID
source query, source system, and observed time
structured metrics and recency signals
apparent primary lane and format mix
contact-readiness signal
coarse-screen decision
unknowns that require fine selection
```

The coarse result is a research queue. It earns deeper review by satisfying the structured must-haves and presenting a plausible route to the business objective.

### 2.3 Pass B — fine selection (high precision, richer evidence)

Spend detail and browser-review effort on a smaller, purposeful subset:

- high-potential candidates;
- candidates with important missing information;
- boundary cases near a threshold;
- candidates whose structured metrics conflict with the content signal;
- supply that is strategically important to the market or scene.

Use creator detail data and, when available, browser inspection of the channel, About page, recent videos, titles, thumbnails, descriptions, comments, and visible cooperation context. Browser review is a first-class evidence source for this pass because the question is whether the partnership will feel natural to the audience, not merely whether a row passes a filter.

For each candidate, inspect at least **3–5 representative pieces of recent content**, normally within **90 days** and with preference for continued activity within **60 days**. Separate long-form, Shorts, live replays, and other formats. Record the recent comparable view range or median, explain anomalies such as a single breakout, and compare commercial and non-commercial content where useful.

Answer the business questions:

1. Does the creator repeatedly operate in a scene where the product can be used truthfully?
2. Does the audience, market, and language fit the target behavior?
3. Can the creator explain or demonstrate the relevant value in their natural format?
4. Is recent performance representative and sufficiently consistent for this objective?
5. Do authenticity, brand safety, prior partnerships, and production signals support a workable relationship?
6. What specific cooperation idea would make the selection credible?

Record the supporting links, observation time, interpretation, confidence, and the largest unresolved risk. When a browser or detail source is unavailable, label the decision provisional and name the smallest follow-up that would increase confidence.

### 2.4 Separate fit from readiness

Return two related judgments:

| Dimension | Result examples | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Creator fit | confirmed, provisional, weak | likelihood of advancing the current objective |
| Contact readiness | ready, contact pending, identity unverified | ability to take the next communication action |

Use public, verifiable business contacts and record how ownership was confirmed. A qualified creator with contact pending remains in the portfolio and receives a contact task. A contact-ready creator with weak fit remains low priority even if outreach would be easy.

### 2.5 Decide when to search again

Use fine-selection findings to diagnose the supply problem:

- many coarse candidates fail the same content test → revise the query or creator hypothesis;
- metrics pass but scenes are unnatural → change the content angle or lane definition;
- strong fits lack contact routes → allocate a bounded contact-research batch;
- recent performance is broadly below the target → revisit the threshold, market, format, or objective with the user;
- duplicate rate is high → improve identity matching and source coverage before adding volume.

Scale only when the bounded sample shows that the hypothesis and qualification evidence are working. A smaller, well-supported shortlist is a valid stage result.

## 3. Build a useful shortlist

Give each shortlisted creator a role in the proposed portfolio: proven category voice, adjacent audience, dependable producer, conversion-oriented specialist, or controlled experiment. Show why the role serves the objective rather than ranking by a universal score.

A shortlist entry should include:

- creator identity, platform, market, language, and primary lane;
- the concrete cooperation scene;
- recent representative evidence and its freshness;
- fit decision and confidence;
- contact readiness and next qualification action;
- principal risk, trade-off, and alternative.

Use follower scale as a reach-planning input. Use recent comparable content, audience relevance, scene credibility, complete cost, and execution likelihood to decide priority. If the project requests a number that supply cannot support at the required quality, report the gap and propose the next hypothesis.

## 4. Create and manage outreach

### 4.1 Write a credible first contact

The first contact seeks a clear reply. Build it from:

1. a truthful, specific reason the creator was selected;
2. the product and the creator's real content scene;
3. a value exchange meaningful to both parties;
4. a proposed collaboration format at the right level of detail;
5. one low-friction question or next step;
6. a clear sender identity and response route.

Let the primary lane choose the story: a technical reviewer, outdoor creator, student creator, shopping creator, and seasonal-gifting creator should receive different use scenes even when the product is identical. Keep price, rights, exclusivity, and delivery promises within the approved brief.

### 4.2 Prepare contact and send

For a qualified creator without a ready contact, check the channel's public contact area, linked website or media kit, and clearly associated public profiles in that order. Verify ownership through the channel identity, domain, or linked account. Preserve a contact-pending record while the fit evidence remains useful.

Before an external send, confirm the approved template or message version, recipient identity, sender, subject, body, links or attachments, copied recipients, and applicable consent or suppression state. After sending, read back the task or message and record the actual result.

### 4.3 Follow up under an approved cadence

Use campaign timing, channel norms, creator value, and prior interaction to choose the cadence. A practical starting point is a first follow-up after **3–5 business days**, a second after another **5–7 business days**, and a short close-the-loop note. Once the user approves the rule, continue routine follow-ups for eligible creators without re-requesting approval for each identical action. A reply, opt-out, bounce, or material change moves the creator to response handling. After a substantive reply, prepare a response for the user's confirmation unless an approved rule explicitly covers that reply class.

## 5. Turn replies into decisions

Treat an inbound message as new business evidence.

1. Detect new messages and deduplicate on a stable message ID.
2. Preserve the complete original text and message time.
3. Classify the response: interested, conditional, information-seeking, price/terms requested, later timing, declined, automatic, bounced, or another useful state.
4. Summarize the intent and extract the terms that change the next decision.
5. Re-check creator fit, recent content, data credibility, and risk before client submission or negotiation.
6. Choose the next action and authority; ask for confirmation when it changes a material promise.

Extract at least:

- platform, format, deliverables, quantity, placement, and duration;
- fee, currency, tax, expenses, incentives, deposit, and payment timing;
- usage, ownership, paid media, whitelisting, territory, and duration;
- category exclusivity and duration;
- topic, product, schedule, revisions, approval, cancellation, make-good, shipping, and reporting;
- the user's or client's unresolved decision.

An interested reply is a productive conversation result. A confirmed cooperation requires a complete, approved package.

## 6. Negotiate the complete cooperation

Evaluate the package as a whole:

```text
complete cost = base fee + rights + exclusivity + tax + logistics + production support + payment cost
estimated CPV = complete cost / expected effective views
```

Before replying, define the desired package, acceptable limits, best alternative, unresolved facts, and the reason each concession could create value. Trade variables deliberately—scope, format, rights, exclusivity, timing, revisions, bundle size, or payment structure—so a price movement has a corresponding change in value or risk.

Use current comparable evidence for market guidance. When it is absent, state the uncertainty and reason from expected contribution, recent performance, alternatives, complete cost, and downside. Keep the relationship workable while advancing the largest unresolved issue.

For a material commitment, present:

- the proposed package and total economics;
- evidence and confidence;
- target position, limits, and trade-offs;
- remaining risks and missing terms;
- a practical alternative;
- the exact decision the user needs to make before acceptance is sent.

## 7. Fulfill and publish

Turn approved terms into an observable checklist with owners, inputs, product or access, milestones, review points, publication window, disclosure, tracking, payment conditions, and completion evidence.

Give the creator accurate product facts and mandatory requirements while preserving the authentic voice that justified selection. Before publication, verify the agreed content, links, disclosure, tracking, rights, timing, and approval state. After publication, confirm the public asset, capture its identifier and timestamp, and monitor agreed windows.

When delivery changes, assess the effect on the objective, economics, rights, and schedule; update the plan and obtain the authority required for a changed commitment.

## 8. Measure and learn

Choose metrics from the primary objective:

- awareness: effective reach, views, CPV, search or lift signals;
- education: watch time, retention, qualified comments, landing-page visits;
- traffic: clicks, CTR, qualified sessions;
- sales: orders, revenue, CVR, CPA, ROAS, and attribution window;
- content asset: usable files, rights duration, reuse value;
- relationship: response quality, on-time delivery, repeatability, and cost trend.

Separate delivery metrics, audience response, traffic or conversion, commercial outcome, and longer-term learning. Label attribution confidence and data limitations. Compare results with the original hypothesis, identify supported and contradicted assumptions, and choose the next support, reuse, renegotiation, pause, or test decision.

## 9. Maintain a verifiable record

For each meaningful stage result or state change, retain:

```text
goal and stage
hypothesis or decision question
creator/relationship identity and stable ID
evidence: source, observed time, and what it supports
decision, confidence, and unresolved risk
next action, owner, and authority/confirmation
observed result and current state
```

Keep current dynamic state in the system that owns it. Keep dated snapshots as historical evidence and promote a lesson to reusable guidance only when repeated results or experienced operator review show where it transfers. The user-facing response can stay concise; the record should make the decision and its evidence reproducible.
